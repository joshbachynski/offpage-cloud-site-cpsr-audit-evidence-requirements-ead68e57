CPSR audit evidence requirements for a clear, defensible audit package

CPSR audit evidence requirements are the documents, records, and controls a contractor should be able to show during a Contractor Purchasing System Review. They prove that purchasing practices are written, followed, and supported by traceable records. A strong evidence set helps a reviewer verify compliance quickly and consistently.

If you are building a review-ready file, this page gives a practical starting point for CPSR audit evidence requirements and how to organize them so they are easy to follow. The goal is not to overwhelm the reviewer with paper; it is to show a complete, logical trail from policy to practice to result. When evidence is well selected and clearly labeled, it becomes much easier to demonstrate that the purchasing system is controlled and operating as intended.

  • Show the written policy or procedure.
  • Show the transaction record or file sample.
  • Show approvals, reviews, and exceptions.
  • Show how the file ties back to the system requirement.

CPSR audit evidence requirements that define a review-ready package

A review-ready package contains evidence that is current, relevant, and traceable to the control being tested. The reviewer should be able to move from the procedure to the file to the supporting record without guessing. Evidence is strongest when it demonstrates both compliance and actual use.

In a Contractor Purchasing System Review, auditors typically look for more than a policy manual. They want proof that the company’s purchasing process works in practice, not just on paper. That means your evidence should include samples that reflect normal operations, approvals that match authority levels, and records that show how exceptions were handled. If a file is incomplete, the reviewer may question whether the control is truly operating.

A useful way to think about the package is in layers. The first layer is the rule: policy, procedure, delegation, or work instruction. The second layer is the application: purchase requests, solicitations, quotes, purchase orders, receiving records, and subcontractor documentation. The third layer is the oversight: internal reviews, management approvals, corrective actions, or training records that show the system is maintained. Together, these layers create a clear narrative that a reviewer can follow.

CPSR audit evidence requirements for core document categories and file samples

Core evidence usually includes policies, procedures, purchasing files, approval records, vendor information, and any documents showing price or source selection. The exact sample set depends on the purchasing activity being tested. A complete file should support how the decision was made and who authorized it.

Most audit evidence falls into a few recognizable categories. Policy documents explain what should happen. File samples show what did happen. Approval logs and signatures show who reviewed the action. Training records or system screenshots may show that the process is communicated and used. If your organization relies on electronic workflows, those records are just as important as signed paper forms, because the reviewer may need to confirm control in the system itself.

File samples should be selected carefully. A good sample is representative, recent enough to reflect current practice, and complete enough to show the full chain of events. If the evidence is for a high-value or high-risk purchase, the file may need extra support, such as source justification or competition documentation. If the file involves an exception, the reason for the exception should be visible and approved.

Reviewers also pay attention to consistency. A single purchase order may not tell much on its own, but several files with the same missing fields can signal a broader control problem. That is why evidence should be assembled with an eye toward repeatability. It should show that the process works across different buyers, commodities, and approval paths, not only in one ideal example.

CPSR audit evidence requirements for traceability, approval, and vendor control

Traceability means the reviewer can follow each purchasing action from request to award to receipt or closeout. Approval evidence should match the company’s delegation of authority and internal controls. Vendor control evidence should show how suppliers are selected, evaluated, and monitored.

Traceability is often the difference between a file that is acceptable and a file that raises questions. The reviewer should be able to see how the need was identified, how competition or source selection was handled, who approved the decision, and whether the received product or service matched what was ordered. Missing links in that chain can create uncertainty even when the purchase itself was reasonable.

Approval evidence should be unambiguous. If the company requires a manager, buyer, and quality or program review at certain thresholds, those approvals should appear in the file or in the workflow record. The authorization should also be current. An outdated delegation matrix or an approval from someone without authority can weaken the file, even if other parts are complete.

Vendor control evidence can include supplier evaluation records, conflict-of-interest checks, business status documentation, or performance notes. The point is to show that supplier selection is not arbitrary. Where applicable, the file should also reflect flowdown requirements, terms and conditions, and any relevant special instructions. If the purchasing system is expected to control supplier quality or delivery risk, the evidence should show how those controls are applied.

CPSR audit evidence requirements for organizing records and avoiding common gaps

The best evidence set is organized by requirement, then by transaction, then by support record. Common gaps include missing approvals, incomplete quotes, undated records, and files that do not explain exceptions. Clean indexing and naming reduce review friction and help the auditor confirm the record quickly.

Organization matters because auditors must be able to navigate the record efficiently. A labeled index, consistent file naming, and a standard folder structure can make a large review much easier to manage. When the evidence is grouped by purchasing function or control area, it is simpler to see whether each requirement has a matching record. That structure also helps internal teams find and reuse evidence during future reviews.

Common gaps are often simple, but they can have an outsized impact. A missing date can make a record appear unreliable. A quote without a basis for award can leave the selection decision unclear. A signed form without the associated transaction can make the approval look disconnected. Small omissions like these are worth fixing early because they are harder to explain later.

It also helps to prepare a short explanation for unusual files. If a purchase was sole source, expedited, or otherwise nonstandard, the file should say why. If a record lives in another system, the evidence package should point to the source instead of forcing the reviewer to search. Clear cross-references save time and support confidence in the purchasing system.

CPSR audit evidence requirements for preparing internal teams before the review

Preparation means training the people who own the records and confirming that sample files are complete before the review starts. Internal teams should know where evidence lives, what a good file looks like, and how to explain exceptions. A dry run often reveals missing support that can still be corrected in time.

Even a strong purchasing system can struggle if the team is not ready to present its evidence clearly. Buyers, managers, subcontract administrators, and quality staff should know what documents are expected and where those documents are stored. They should also know how to describe the process in plain language, because reviewers often ask how the file was built and why a certain path was taken.

A practical preparation step is to test a few sample files before the review. Check whether each file has the policy link, approval trail, supplier record, and final outcome. Confirm that exceptions are documented and that the people named in the file are available to explain their role. This kind of preparation does more than reduce stress; it helps identify weak spots before the reviewer does.

When the evidence package is accurate, organized, and current, it supports a smoother review and a stronger system story. The reviewer can see not only what the company says it does, but also what it can prove through records. That is the real value of careful audit preparation: it turns purchasing control into something visible, understandable, and defensible.

Strong audit evidence is not a pile of documents; it is a connected record that shows policy, action, and oversight working together.
